**Summary (open-ad-sim neuropathology and imaging calibration targets, checked 2026-10-19).** Most of the headline numbers exist and are quoted correctly: Gómez-Isla 1996 (whole entorhinal cortex −32% at CDR 0.5), Price 2001 (entorhinal −35%, CA1 −46%), von Gunten 2006 (CA1 −26%), Scheff 2007 (CA1 synapses −18% in MCI, −55% in mild AD), Scheff 2011 (inferior temporal synapses −36% in aMCI), Strom 2022 (spatial r with tau 0.55–0.76 vs amyloid 0.22–0.28), and the SV2A papers (Chen 2018, Mecca 2020). West found no significant CA1 loss in preclinical AD. **(14a, partial) LATE.** 40% is the *average* any-stage LATE-NC prevalence in 13 autopsy cohorts with a mean age at death of 88. ### 14a. LATE-NC prevalence assumption
- **Nelson PT, Dickson DW, Trojanowski JQ, et al.** Limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE): consensus working group report. *Brain* 2019;142(6):1503–1527. doi:10.1093/brain/awz099 (PMC6536849 full text).

  - "Among subjects autopsied past 80 years of age, most studies indicate that >20% of brains had pathological features consistent with LATE-NC"; "ranging from 5% to 50% of brains". The figure legend (NACC) adds: "The percentage of subjects with LATE-NC is still >20% in each age group" (including <75).
- **Nelson PT, Brayne C, Flanagan ME, et al.** Frequency of LATE neuropathologic change across the spectrum of Alzheimer's disease neuropathology: combined data from 13 community-based or population-based autopsy cohorts. *Acta Neuropathol* 2022;144(1):27–44. doi:10.1007/s00401-022-02444-1 (PMC9552938 full text).

### 14c. "Rates double every ~15 years of age" — NOT FOUND
- **Fraser MA, Shaw ME, Cherbuin N.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of longitudinal hippocampal atrophy in healthy human ageing. *NeuroImage* 2015;112:364–374. doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2015.03.035 (PubMed 25800208): "0.38% per year … for studies with mean age <55 years …, 0.98% … for 55 to <70 years …, and 1.12% … for ≥70 years"; meta-regression +0.0263%/yr per year of age. Rates rise steeply from midlife but only by about 14% from the 55–70 band to the ≥70 band.
- **Jack CR Jr, Wiste HJ, Weigand SD, et al.** Predicting future rates of tau accumulation on PET. *Brain* 2020;143(10):3136–3150. doi:10.1093/brain/awaa248 (PubMed 33094327): "In the cognitively unimpaired group, only higher baseline amyloid PET was a significant independent predictor of higher tau accumulation rates"; "In the cognitively impaired group, younger age (P = 0.02) … [was a] significant independent predictor[] of higher tau accumulation rates."
- **Fiford CM et al.** Patterns of progressive atrophy vary with age in Alzheimer's disease patients. *Neurobiol Aging* 2018;63:22–32. doi:10.1016/j.neurobiolaging.2017.11.002 (PubMed 29220823): "younger AD patients had significantly faster atrophy rates in the bilateral precuneus, parietal, and superior temporal lobes."
- **Jorm AF, Jolley D.** The incidence of dementia: a meta-analysis. *Neurology* 1998;51(3):728–733. doi:10.1212/wnl.51.3.728 (PubMed 9748017): "The incidence of both dementia and AD rose exponentially up to the age of 90 years". This is population incidence, not per-person progression rate; the abstract gives no doubling time.
- **Assessment:** no source supports a 15-year doubling of per-person tau or neurodegeneration rates. In symptomatic amyloid-positive people the age effect runs the other way. The assumption should be stated as such, ideally with a sensitivity analysis.
