# Night of 2026-11-21: Who stays well - and is deep sleep a shield or a gauge?

Resilience lens (people who carry the pathology but not the disease), starting from sleep, and
revisiting `nonprogressor-rate-plus-synapse`. Earlier sleep nights asked whether poor sleep causes
the disease (weak evidence) and whether sleep drugs slow it (parked). Tonight asked something
different: at a given load of amyloid and tau, do people with intact deep sleep cope better, or is
deep sleep only a readout of damage?

## Questions asked

- At the same pathology, do people with more deep sleep do better - and has anyone followed them
  over time?
- What do the sleep-and-wake centres look like in brains that carried heavy pathology without
  dementia?
- Do people who naturally need little sleep tell us whether sleep hours matter at all?
- (Revisit) To predict who stays well, how much does a person's own rate of tau build-up add to
  its level, and how good must a reserve marker be?
- New lens questions added: cause of tolerance or readout of it; the validity bar for a reserve
  marker; and whether a rate of change is real or just a second look at the level.

## What I found (with sources)

**Deep sleep as a shield: a consistent pattern in small one-night studies, no long-term test.**

- **The founding study:** in 62 cognitively normal older adults, more slow-wave activity went with
  better next-day memory only in the amyloid-positive half (B = 2.69, p = 0.019,
  10.1186/s12916-023-02811-z, abstract checked). It was one night, not adjusted for tau, and the
  amyloid-positive group had *more* slow-wave activity, which fits compensation or over-excitability
  as well as reserve (as reported from the full text).
- **Against:** a pooled analysis of five cohorts found no link between sleep architecture, including
  slow-wave power, and later dementia (4,657 people, 998 cases, 10.1093/sleep/zsaf129, checked).
- **Tau makes slow waves abnormal** (10.1038/s41593-026-02415-9, as reported), which makes deep
  sleep a pathway from tau to memory loss, not a buffer against it.
- **The decisive study does not exist:** nobody has tested whether baseline deep sleep predicts who
  stays well among unimpaired people with both amyloid and tau (as reported).

**Brains that resisted: the brainstem was spared.**

- Among people at the heaviest cortical tau stage, those without dementia had significantly less
  tau in the locus coeruleus and raphe nuclei than those with dementia (Nun Study, 15 versus 15,
  10.1186/s40478-025-02167-w, abstract checked).
- More surviving locus coeruleus neurons predicted slower decline independent of other pathology
  (165 people, 10.1212/WNL.0b013e3182897103, as reported).
- Better locus coeruleus integrity on imaging went with more slow-wave activity (58 people) and
  less broken sleep (39 people) (as reported).
- No study has looked at sleep itself, or at orexin cells or the body clock, in resilient brains.

**Natural short sleepers.** Two variants that let people sleep four to six hours reduced tau and
plaque in mouse models (10.1016/j.isci.2022.103964, abstract checked), and a third restored dream
sleep and cut tau spread (10.1073/pnas.2221686120, as reported). There is no human evidence on
dementia in carriers. Gene-level look-ups in two open, no-login portals showed nothing, but those
tests cannot see the specific rare variants (as reported).

**Needing more sleep is a symptom, not a coping cost.** Longer and more frequent naps predicted
dementia (hazard ratio about 1.2 per SD, 1,401 people), napping more than doubled its yearly rise
after diagnosis, and genetic studies point from disease to napping, not the reverse
(10.1002/alz.12636; 10.3233/JAD-220497; as reported). An idea built on "sleep need as the price
of coping" was dropped before entry.

**Raising deep sleep on purpose (as reported).** Sound stimulation raises slow waves in small
studies; no completed trial has tested whether the benefit depends on how much pathology a person
has, which is what a true shield predicts. One registered trial stratifies by blood p-tau217 and
could (NCT07529015, 114 people, not yet recruiting). A drug that raises slow-wave sleep in older
adults gave no memory benefit in a small study of young men.

**Who stays well (the revisit).**

- **Most people with amyloid alone do; half of those with tau too do not.** At about 3.5 years, 91%
  of amyloid-positive, tau-negative unimpaired people were still unimpaired, against about half of
  those positive for both (1,325 people, 10.1038/s41591-022-02049-x; hazard ratios checked, the
  percentages as reported from the full text).
- **The level beats the rate.** In 761 amyloid-positive people, baseline tau imaging was the strong
  determinant of later decline and the change in tau imaging contributed marginally
  (10.1002/alz.70435, abstract checked). Blood-marker slopes did not beat baseline levels either
  (10.1001/jamaneurol.2025.2974, as reported).
- **A two-year personal rate is hard to measure.** Scan test-retest error is about 1.5-3% for one
  tracer and 4-9% for others; yearly change in amyloid-positive unimpaired people is about 0.012
  SUVR with an observed spread of 0.026 that includes noise (10.1093/brain/awy059, as reported).
  The assistant's own calculation puts the real share of a two-year rate at 20-60%.
- **The best reserve marker is a spinal-fluid synapse ratio.** It predicted conversion with a
  hazard ratio of 3.0 per SD in unimpaired people with both pathologies and explained 27% of the
  variation in impairment beyond the standard spinal-fluid markers (10.1038/s41591-025-03565-2,
  abstract checked). It rests on one study, has no published reliability data, and does not work
  in blood (as reported).
- **No resilience marker has been raised by any intervention in people** (as reported).

## Modeling: what a second scan and a reserve marker each add

`scripts/nonprogressor_two_marker_2026_11_21.py`: 40,000 synthetic amyloid-positive people whose
tau grows at a personal rate and who progress when it passes a personal tolerance. Two scenarios
bracket how much people truly differ in rate. Scores are AUC for predicting who stays well for
five years (0.5 is a coin toss), at a scan error of 0.04 SUVR.

| What is measured | People differ little in rate | People differ a lot in rate |
|---|---|---|
| One scan | 0.825 | 0.787 |
| One scan, repeated straight away | 0.841 | 0.797 |
| Scan now and in two years (level + rate) | 0.848 | 0.855 |
| Scan now and in four years | 0.856 | 0.887 |
| Level and rate known perfectly | 0.872 | 0.904 |
| One scan + reserve marker, validity 0.5 | 0.866 | 0.812 |
| One scan + reserve marker, validity 0.7 | 0.902 | 0.845 |

- **Most of what a second scan adds is a sharper level.** When people differ little in rate, a scan
  two years later beats an immediate repeat by only 0.007.
- **Whether the rate matters depends on how much people differ.** With a wide spread it adds about
  0.06. The published finding that the level dominates fits the low-spread scenario better.
- **A reserve marker needs a validity of about 0.5 to add 0.03-0.04, and about 0.7 to match a
  perfect rate.** Slow-wave power correlated about 0.55 with cognition in healthy older adults,
  which is not the same thing as tracking reserve.
- My first version of this model had a reading written before the numbers; the numbers contradicted
  it and the script was restructured around the comparison with an immediate repeat.
- Limits: assumed parameters; progression here depends only on tau and a hidden tolerance, which
  favours both markers; the contrast between scenarios is the result, not the third decimal.

## Revisited: nonprogressor-rate-plus-synapse -> PARKED

The test as stated paired a synaptic marker with a personal tau rate. The rate half does not earn
its cost: evidence and model agree that it adds little beyond the level unless people differ much
more in rate than published data suggest, and it takes years to measure. The synaptic half stands
but is thin and needs spinal fluid. The two successors below keep what survives: sharpen the level
cheaply, and look for a reserve marker that does not need a spinal tap.

## New ideas

1. **brainstem-sparing-sleep-readout** (understand, observational) - resilience may live in spared
   brainstem nuclei, with deep sleep as their readable output. Kill: deep sleep still buffers tau's
   effect on cognition after brainstem integrity is in the model.
2. **slow-wave-power-reserve-marker** (detect, observational) - test at-home slow-wave power as the
   reserve half of a stay-well test. Kill: it adds less than 0.02 AUC to tau level.
3. **level-twice-before-rate** (detect, in-silico; investigating) - for prognosis, measure the level
   twice now, or average several blood draws, before waiting years for a rate. Kill: level plus
   slope clearly beats an averaged baseline in data with three or more visits.
4. **short-sleeper-variant-natural-experiment** (understand, preclinical) - natural short sleepers
   test whether sleep hours matter; mouse data say the variants protect. Kill: carriers do no
   better, or the mouse result fails in a second laboratory. Probably too rare to test in people.

  neither confirms nor weakens a catalogue idea.
