# Night of 2026-11-19: What carries the decline that continues after plaque is removed?

Causal-chain lens for people who already have symptoms, revisiting
`sleep-regularity-vs-duration`. The earlier causal-chain night (2026-11-02) looked at genetics and
approved drugs; tonight asked a narrower question. Plaque-removing antibodies slow decline by about
a quarter to a third. Where does the rest come from, and can trials already run tell us?

Two research assistants ran in parallel and both finished. I re-checked the figures that drive
tonight's conclusions against abstracts or published summaries; those are marked "checked".

> Research hypotheses, not medical advice; nothing identifies a real person. Claims carry a
> DOI/PMID/NCT. "As reported" marks items from an assistant's report that I did not check myself.

## Questions asked

- Which link of amyloid -> tau -> nerve-cell loss -> symptoms is weakest once symptoms exist?
- Three accounts of the remaining decline - other brain diseases, downstream processes that no
  longer need amyloid, or slow clearance - what does each predict that the others do not?
- Could a re-analysis of stored trial samples separate them, and with what power?
- What does this month's failed or odd result (the tau-lowering drug's dose pattern) say: wrong
  target, wrong dose, or noise?
- (Revisit) Does irregular sleep damage the brain, or does a damaged brain sleep irregularly?
- New lens questions added: fixed share or fixed slice; what the shape of benefit over time
  implies; and the power of a subgroup before reading its null.

## What I found (with sources)

**Mixed disease is the rule, but it cannot be the whole answer.**

- In community autopsy cohorts Alzheimer's pathology occurred alone in only 9% of brains (n=1,079,
  10.1002/ana.25123, read in full by the assistant).
- A Lewy-body seed test in spinal fluid is positive in about a fifth of amyloid-positive
  symptomatic people in one clinic cohort (98 of 475, 10.1038/s41591-023-02449-7) and in 23% at the
  mild-impairment stage and 45% at dementia in another (10.1186/s13024-025-00822-3); positives
  decline faster. There is still no validated test for TDP-43 disease in living people.
- Against a large role: all eleven measured pathologies together explain about 43% of the variation
  in late-life decline, so more than half is unexplained by anything pathologists measure
  (n=1,164, 10.1093/brain/awab092, read in full by the assistant).

**No antibody trial has yet been analysed by co-pathology.** None was found for any of the four
large trials. A first analysis, in a spinal-fluid substudy of the lecanemab trial, was scheduled
for presentation today (as reported from a pre-meeting press release; I could not find the listing
or any result). A separate trial of donanemab in people with both amyloid and Lewy-body disease is
recruiting (NCT07589595, 350 people, results from 2028; registry entry, as reported).

**The absolute benefit of plaque removal looks the same at low and high tau.** In the donanemab
trial the benefit on the CDR-SB scale was 0.67 points (0.40 to 0.95) in people with low-to-medium
tau and 0.69 (0.20 to 1.19, post hoc) in people with high tau, although the high-tau group declines
much faster (10.1001/jama.2023.13239; both figures checked). On the trial's other main scale the
high-tau benefit was much smaller and not significant, so this holds for one scale only.

**Benefit and amyloid lowering across trials.** Trial-level correlations between amyloid removed
and clinical benefit run about 0.5-0.8 on a handful of trials, and no study gives an
individual-level "share explained" for amyloid, blood tau markers or tau imaging (as reported).

**Conference and recent results (as reported; press releases unless stated).**

- **Tau-lowering antisense drug (NCT05399888, 416 people):** the primary dose-response test failed.
  Tau fell more at higher doses, yet the clinical signal was strongest at the lowest dose: about
  26%, 14% and 9% slowing from lowest to highest dose at 76 weeks, and about 38% at the lowest dose
  at 96 weeks (not significant; the placebo group had crossed over). I confirmed the direction from
  the funder's and the charity's statements, not the numbers.
- **Other failures this season:** an amyloid-aggregate-dissolving pill, 40 Hz stimulation, a
  cortisol-pathway drug and a PDE5 inhibitor all missed their primary endpoints.
- **Brain-shuttle antibody, the second tau antibody and the next plaque-remover:** no clinical
  numbers found. Nothing is regraded tonight.

## Modeling: three accounts of the remaining decline

`scripts/residual_decline_decomposition_2026_11_19.py`, built on published group-level numbers:
the lecanemab trial's 0.45-point benefit at 18 months and 0.17 (0.05 to 0.29) at 6 months
(10.1056/NEJMoa2212948; the 6-month figure checked against a published summary), and the extension
study's 0.52, 1.01 and 1.75 points at 18, 36 and 48 months against an outside comparison group
(conference abstract, 10.1002/alz70861_108905, abstract checked).

**1. The shape of benefit over time.** Each account is tuned to give 0.45 at 18 months.

| Account | 6 months | 36 months | 48 months | Share of decline amyloid drives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fast switch: a fixed part of decline turns off within weeks | 0.15 | 1.04 | 1.52 | 27% |
| Tracks plaque still present, which clears over months | 0.08 | 1.29 | 1.99 | 57% |
| Same, with a nine-month downstream delay | 0.03 | 1.94 | 3.32 | 115% (impossible) |
| Reported | 0.17 | 1.01 | 1.75 | |

- The delayed account is excluded: it needs amyloid to drive more than all of the decline and
  predicts almost no benefit at 6 months.
- The fast switch sits closest to the reported numbers, but the 6-month interval includes the
  plaque-tracking account too.
- The late columns depend on my assumption that natural decline speeds up (1.3 and 1.6 times);
  without it the first two rows read 0.90/1.20 and 1.10/1.54. The extension has no placebo group
  and counts only people who stayed on treatment.
- So the same 27% headline is compatible with amyloid driving roughly a quarter of decline or more
  than half of it. The visit-by-visit trial data could narrow this.

**2. A ceiling for the co-pathology account.** If other diseases carry 40% of decline that the
drug cannot touch, amyloid would have to drive 45% of the Alzheimer-specific decline under the
fast-switch account and 94% under the plaque-tracking account. Co-pathology could close most of the
gap only if benefit tracks plaque.

**3. Can stored samples tell?** Power to detect that marker-positive people get no benefit at all,
using the spread implied by the trial's own confidence interval (SD 2.34 points) and assuming 25%
are marker-positive:

| Data | Power |
|---|---|
| Spinal-fluid substudy, 281-410 people | 15-20% |
| One whole trial | 64% |
| Two trials pooled | 91% |

A partial difference (0.30 versus 0.50 points) has 12-20% power even pooled. **Prediction recorded
before seeing today's conference result: a substudy finding of "no difference by Lewy-body status"
would be uninformative.** The substudy size is from secondary sources.

Limits: the clearance time, the delay, the speed-up of natural decline and the marker-positive
share are assumed. This says what to look for in the data, not what is true.

## Revisited: sleep-regularity-vs-duration -> PARKED

- **Regularity is the weakest rhythm predictor.** Across about ten cohort analyses the classic
  regularity measure was null in several and pointed the wrong way for Alzheimer's in one
  (10.1186/s13195-024-01411-0); rhythm strength and fragmentation carry the signal (as reported).
- **The two analyses of the newer regularity index disagree** on the shape of the curve and neither
  tested prediction formally (10.1212/WNL.0000000000208029; 10.1186/s12889-025-21649-z, as reported).
- **Direction leans to consequence.** Yearly rhythm decline doubled or more after a diagnosis of
  mild impairment and doubled again after dementia (n=1,401, 10.1016/s2666-7568(20)30015-5,
  checked). Genetic studies find Alzheimer's risk shifting sleep, with no evidence of sleep causing
  Alzheimer's (10.1212/WNL.0000000000010463, checked). The body-clock and sleep-promoting cell
  groups degenerate in the disease (as reported).
- **The slow-wave version cannot be measured as stated.** Night-to-night variation in scored deep
  sleep was 47% in healthy older adults, mostly noise; slow-wave power was stable and was the only
  measure tied to cognition (49 people, 302 nights, 10.1093/sleep/zsaf329, checked).
- **In symptomatic people nobody has removed the cause.** A pooled analysis of light-therapy trials
  in dementia found no change in regularity or fragmentation (13 trials, PMID 34788794, as
  reported), and no trial has changed an Alzheimer's marker by regularising sleep.

One point in its favour: in small studies of at-risk adults, night-to-night variability related to
tau and amyloid imaging where averages did not (10.1002/alz.71228; marginal p-values, as reported).
Re-open on a head-to-head test with a formal prediction statistic, or a study using slow-wave power
variability with imaging.

A side result for `amplitude-is-volume`: others have partly run our test. In the public US survey
data a rhythm measure lost its link to processing speed after adjusting for sleep duration and
total activity (10.1038/s41598-024-64309-9, checked). That idea's open-data analysis is now a
replication, not a first test.

## New ideas

1. **fixed-slice-not-fixed-share** (understand, RCT) - plaque removal subtracts the same absolute
   amount at every tau stage; "percent slowing" shrinks by arithmetic. Kill: absolute benefit falls
   with tau on most scales.
2. **benefit-timing-shape-test** (understand, RCT; investigating) - fit the visit-by-visit trial
   differences to a fast switch versus plaque-tracking. Kill: the published visits cannot separate
   them.
3. **copathology-pooled-interaction-test** (understand, observational) - test whether a second
   brain disease blunts benefit using a baseline-scan marker in every participant, pooled across
   trials. Kill: equal benefit in a test with about 90% power.
4. **tau-antisense-dose-inversion** (slow, RCT) - benefit may saturate at low dose while a cost
   rises with dose, as with the same drug class in Huntington's disease (10.1056/NEJMc2300400,
   secondary summaries only). Kill: no dose ordering of nerve-injury markers and no advantage for a
   lower dose. The plain alternative is noise.
